A ledger that nobody owns, and anybody can check.

Contracts signed with keys, payments tagged with what they were for, and a record that a court, a statistics office or a rival bank can replay from the first block. This page walks the life of one transaction. Every step is something you can run.

1

A payment becomes final

Ana pays the café. Nobody is in charge, so who decides it happened? Four validators do, together, and only when more than two thirds of them agree twice.

In the repository: jurisledger integration runs exactly this, with signed votes, on a simulated hostile network; jurisledger cluster runs it as four processes.

2

The network is attacked

An attacker cannot forge a vote, so they do the one thing left: delay messages. One round of voting breaks. The second round, with a lock, holds. Watch both under the same attack.

In the repository: jurisledger asynchrony runs this attack plus 1,000 random ones with a lying validator. Single-phase forks in some; two-phase never does.

A contract is sealed

The words stay off the ledger. What goes on it is a fingerprint of the words, and each party's signature over that exact fingerprint. Edit the text below and watch what happens to a signed agreement.

not signed

not signed
Draft
A contract is a draft until every named party has signed the same fingerprint. Signatures bind to the fingerprint, not to the title, so changing one character afterwards invalidates nothing on the ledger — it simply produces a different document that nobody has signed.
4

Who is anyone

No single registrar decides who exists. An account counts as verified when enough independent issuers have vouched for it. Press issuers to attest; one of them is corrupt.

What the account can do

    In the repository: jurisledger identity registers twenty fake firms with one corrupt issuer's help; none of them can sign a contract. Validators then vote the corrupt issuer out and its attestations stop counting at once.

    5

    Hidden amounts, honest books

    A public ledger of every payment would be a surveillance machine. So the amount is hidden inside a commitment, and a proof shows it is a whole number of cents between 0 and 42,949,672.95 without saying which. Try to pay a negative amount.

    Why 32 bits

    The value is split into bits. Each bit gets its own commitment and a proof that it is 0 or 1 — nothing else. The 32 pieces add up to the original commitment. A negative number has no such decomposition, so the proof cannot be built, and the ledger refuses the payment.

    In the repository: jurisledger confidential makes a real payment with two of these proofs and rejects money printing, overspending and replay. A proof is about 11 kB and takes a quarter of a second in pure Python.

    The economy, measured

    Every payment says what it was for, and the ledger refuses tags that do not fit the accounts involved. Gross domestic product then stops being an estimate and becomes a sum. Drag the slider to see the one thing the ledger cannot see.

    2,196,332.41by expenditure
    2,196,332.41by production
    2,196,332.41by income
    Ledger captures 100.0% of true GDP. Three approaches computed from different transactions agree to the cent; that agreement is a consistency check, not a coincidence.

    How the tags become accounts

    In the repository: jurisledger gdp compares the ledger with the simulator's own books and with a stylised survey. Ledger error on what is on it: 0.0%.

    7

    Fraud, found

    Some fraud the ledger makes impossible: forged payments, replays, back-dated contracts. Some it can only make visible. Round-tripping — passing the same money in a circle to inflate revenue — is the second kind. Inject one and run the detector.

    Six firms trade normally. Nothing to see yet.

    In the repository: jurisledger fraud injects four frauds — a ring, structuring under a reporting threshold, invented invoice amounts, and one receivable pledged to two banks — and reports its false positives on a clean economy too.

     

    Run every one of these yourself

    Each claim on this page is a boolean in the test suite. Tested on Ubuntu with Python 3.10 or newer; the only dependency is the cryptography package.

    # get it
    git clone https://github.com/Normansrule/juris-ledger.git && cd juris-ledger
    python3 -m venv .venv && source .venv/bin/activate && pip install -e ".[dev]"

    # the seven steps above, as experiments with checked claims
    jurisledger integration   # 1
    jurisledger asynchrony    # 2
    jurisledger contracts     # 3
    jurisledger identity      # 4
    jurisledger confidential  # 5
    jurisledger gdp           # 6
    jurisledger fraud         # 7

    # then the real thing: four validator processes, one killed and revived
    jurisledger cluster --out cluster
